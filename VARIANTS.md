# Variants tested

Judge criteria, in order: (1) plainly a joke about a meeting that should
have been an email, (2) the laugh lands on its own, turn by the last word,
(3) surprise — not the obvious complaint about length or that it could
have been an email, (4) economy — every word earns its place, no second
sentence, (5) recognition — something true about meetings people sat through.

## A — CHOSEN (93 chars)

> We held a meeting to read the email together, in silence, like a book club with worse snacks.

Why it wins: the surprise is the comparison (criterion 3) — a book club
is an angle nobody expects, and the turn arrives on the last three words
(criterion 2). The recognition is concrete (criterion 5): everyone has sat
through a meeting that just read the email aloud. One sentence, no
throat-clearing, every word earns its place (criterion 4). It names the
meeting and the email explicitly, so it is unmistakably on-topic
(criterion 1).

## B — runner-up (72 chars)

> Someone shared their screen to read aloud the email we were all cc'd on.

Strong recognition and a hard turn on "we were all cc'd on", but the
image is a step more familiar than A's book-club comparison, so it loses
on surprise.

## C (89 chars)

> The meeting minutes were just the email, forwarded, with 'as discussed' added at the top.

Funny and true ("as discussed" is a real office trope), but the joke is
about the minutes, not the meeting itself, so it is a half-step off the
brief's centre.

## D (89 chars)

> The meeting's only action item was scheduling the meeting that would replace the meeting.

Clever recursion, but abstract — it reads like a logic puzzle rather than
a laugh, and "action item" jargon narrows who recognises it instantly.

## Rejected outright

Any line complaining the meeting ran long, or that it "could have been an
email" — the judge brief names exactly that as the obvious angle, so those
lose to any fresh entry. Any two-sentence line — the brief penalises a
second sentence under economy.
